Bolt Officially Withdraws From Tunisia After Money-Laundering Probe

Nearly two months after Tunisian authorities suspended its operations over tax and licensing allegations, ride-hailing app Bolt quietly shut down in Tunisia — with no public explanation and millions in seized assets left behind.

Ride-hailing app Bolt has officially ceased operations in Tunisia, with users now met by a simple in-app message: "Bolt is not available here yet." The final shutdown, which took effect on May 9, 2025, marks the quiet end of a saga that began weeks earlier with a government crackdown on the sector.

How it started

On March 24, 2025, Tunisian authorities announced a suspension targeting Bolt and other ride-hailing platforms operating in the country. The government alleged the companies were engaged in money laundering, tax fraud, and operating without proper legal licensing — accusing them of using opaque corporate structures and shell companies to move funds abroad without transparent oversight.

Enforcement was swift and material: authorities removed Bolt from the national business register, closed its local offices, and seized assets reported at roughly 12 million Tunisian dinars (about $3.9 million).

Weeks of confusion

Despite the March suspension order, Bolt's app kept functioning for several weeks afterward. Drivers continued receiving ride requests and riders in some areas found the service still available, creating a confusing gap between the government's official position and what was actually happening on the ground. Bolt, for its part, had contested the allegations and signaled it intended to appeal.

The company gave no public explanation for the final cutoff — a silent exit after weeks of contesting the very suspension that ultimately ended its Tunisia operations.

What it means for Tunisia's ride-hailing sector

Beyond the specifics of Bolt's case, the episode exposed a deeper issue: Tunisia has no clear, dedicated legal framework for ride-hailing platforms. Without established licensing rules built for the sector, companies operate in legal ambiguity and remain exposed to abrupt regulatory action rather than a predictable compliance path. Reports at the time also referenced government interest in a state-backed ride-hailing alternative, though no such platform had launched as of Bolt's exit.

For fintech and mobility companies more broadly, the Bolt case is a reminder that Tunisia's appetite for regulatory scrutiny extends well beyond banking and payments — and that operating without a sector-specific legal framework carries real risk, even for a well-capitalized international platform.
