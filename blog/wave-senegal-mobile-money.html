How Wave Mobile Money Disrupted Pricing in Senegal

Wave scrapped cash-out fees and charged a flat 1% to send, forcing Senegal's incumbent to follow. Now taxes, a public instant payment rail and a bank are changing the game.

When Wave launched in Senegal in 2018, mobile money users were used to paying to send money and paying again to withdraw their own cash. Wave mobile money dropped deposit and withdrawal fees to zero and charged a flat 1% to send, and within three years the incumbent had to follow. The story has now moved into a second chapter, with a transaction tax, a free central-bank instant payment rail and a banking subsidiary, and both chapters hold lessons for Tunisia.

The 1% model: what Wave mobile money changed

Wave's pricing is simple, and it is the reason for its growth. According to TechCabal, Wave offers:

  • free deposits and withdrawals in its app;
  • a fixed 1% fee on person-to-person transfers;
  • bill payments where the fee is charged to the business, not the user.

TechCabal reported that competitors such as Orange, Free and Expresso were charging between 5% and 10% per transaction. In a cash economy, money moves in and out of wallets all the time: a trader receives a transfer, cashes out at an agent, pays suppliers in cash. A cash-out fee is charged on almost every cycle, so removing it changed the value of a wallet far more than a small cut to the transfer fee would have.

How the incumbent responded

The market leader did not stand still. In November 2021, Senegalese outlet Osiris, citing L'Observateur, reported that Orange Money in Senegal had made withdrawals free and set its sending fee at 0.8%, slightly below Wave's 1%. At that rate, a 5,000 FCFA transfer cost 40 FCFA.

The same report noted that the third-quarter report of Sonatel and Orange attributed lower financial services revenue to strong competition. Agents and distributors, who had lived on withdrawal commissions, also complained loudly about lost income. The price war showed that a challenger can reset what a whole market charges, and that consumers keep most of the gain even when the challenger does not win every customer.

Capital to fund the model

Low prices need deep pockets, especially because agents must hold cash and e-money float. Wave raised a $200 million Series A in September 2021 at a $1.7 billion valuation, becoming Francophone Africa's first unicorn. In June 2025, it added a $137 million debt round led by Rand Merchant Bank, with British International Investment, Finnfund and Norfund, aimed at working capital and expansion. At that time it reported over 20 million monthly active users, more than 150,000 agents and about 3,000 staff across eight markets.

By July 2026, Wave Burkina's director general told the SIPEN-UEMOA fair in Ouagadougou that the company had 25 million active users, 3 million merchants and a presence in 11 countries, according to Wakat Séra. These are company figures, not independently audited. He also said a promotion had temporarily cut the transfer fee to 0.5%.

Chapter two: tax, interoperability and a bank

Three changes in 2025 reshaped the environment Wave helped create.

A tax on mobile money

Senegal's Law No. 2025-17 of 27 September 2025 introduced a 0.5% tax on transfers, payments and withdrawals, capped at 2,000 FCFA per operation, which came into force on 17 December 2025, as reported by Senego. Withdrawals are taxed only when the total withdrawn over 24 hours exceeds 20,000 FCFA; cash deposits and salary payments are exempt. A tax of 0.5% is half of Wave's own fee, which shows how quickly a cheap service can become a revenue base for a stretched government.

A free public rail

On 30 September 2025, the BCEAO, the central bank of the eight-country West African Economic and Monetary Union, launched PI-SPI, an interoperable instant payment platform. When the main selling point of a private network is cheap transfers, a public rail that connects banks and e-money issuers changes the competitive picture.

A banking licence strategy

According to Osiris, Wave Bank Africa SA was incorporated in Côte d'Ivoire in August 2025 with 20 billion FCFA in capital. The outlet linked the move to fee cuts by competitors, the new instant payment system and Senegal's tax, and reported that Wave was not among the first operators authorised on PI-SPI. Wave executives have since said that credit, savings and insurance products are in preparation.

What it means for Tunisia

Tunisia's mobile payment market is still small. Central Bank of Tunisia (BCT) data reported by Ecofin Agency show 8.4 million mobile transactions in 2025, up 81%, worth 1.769 billion dinars. In May 2026, the BCT and Monétique Tunisie introduced the TUNPAY label to make mobile payment services easier to recognise and more interoperable. Several lessons from Senegal apply:

  • Price the cash edges, not just the transfer. In a cash-heavy economy such as Tunisia's, what users pay to get money in and out matters more than the headline transfer fee. See our piece on the Tunisian mobile wallet landscape.
  • Float needs financing. Wave relied on equity first and then development-finance debt to fund agent liquidity. Tunisian wallet operators face the same need under the payment institution regime.
  • Public rails reset the game. Once a central bank offers free, interoperable instant payments, a cheap closed network is no longer enough. Tunisia's own path is covered in instant payments and interoperability.
  • Watch the tax authority. Senegal shows that fast-growing digital flows attract taxes quickly. Any Tunisian strategy built on thin margins should plan for that risk.

Wave's first chapter showed that one disciplined entrant can lower prices for a whole country. Its second chapter shows that pricing alone is not a lasting moat, which is why Wave is now moving into banking, like several of Africa's fintech unicorns.

Frequently asked questions

How much does Wave charge to send money?

Wave charges a flat 1% on person-to-person transfers, and deposits and withdrawals are free, according to TechCabal's 2025 reporting. In July 2026 a Wave Burkina executive said a promotion had temporarily cut the transfer fee to 0.5%. Senegal's 0.5% mobile money tax, in force since December 2025, applies on top of operator fees.

How did Orange Money react to Wave in Senegal?

In November 2021, Orange Money Senegal made withdrawals free and set its sending fee at 0.8%, according to local press. Sonatel and Orange reported lower financial services revenue because of strong competition.

Is Wave available in Tunisia?

No. Wave operates in West and Central Africa and in Uganda, according to company statements in 2026. Tunisia has its own wallets run by banks, La Poste and licensed payment institutions.
